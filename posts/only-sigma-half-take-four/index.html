Chapter 5 ended on a hedge: a precise, checkable hypothesis — k(t) \ge c_0\log^2 t — and an honest admission that I hadn’t verified it, and didn’t know anyone who had. February 2026 opened with a fourth attempt at the same underlying question, from a completely different angle: not curvature this time, but the approximate functional equation. It’s the tightest self-derived argument in the whole project. It also, on inspection, had a real bug — and once that was fixed, it led somewhere I didn’t expect: back to Chapter 5, and past it, to a piece of mathematics with an actual name.

The setup

Fix t and write s=\sigma+it throughout (\sigma = real part, t = height, as everywhere on this site). Recall \zeta(s), for 0<\sigma<1, can be written as a limit of truncated Riemann sums:

\mu(s) := \lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\sum_{n=1}^N n^{-s} - \frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}\right] = \zeta(s).

Truncate it at a finite N instead of taking the limit:

\mu_N(s) := \sum_{n=1}^N n^{-s} - \frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}, \qquad \mu_N(s) = \mu(s) + O(N^{-\sigma}).

Rescale to strip off the trivial part of the decay: \psi_N(s) := N^{s-1}\mu_N(s). This is nothing exotic — it’s exactly the leftover error of a Riemann-sum approximation to an integral, rescaled to sit at a fixed order of magnitude.

The argument

Two facts about \psi_N drive everything:

If \mu(s)\ne0: \mu_N(s)\to\mu(s)\ne0, so |\mu_N(s)|=O(1) and |\psi_N(s)| = N^{\sigma-1}\cdot O(1) = O(N^{\sigma-1}) — the generic decay rate.

If \mu(s)=0 — a genuine zero of \zeta: the leading term vanishes, so \mu_N(s)=O(N^{-\sigma}) instead of O(1), and |\psi_N(s)| = N^{\sigma-1}\cdot O(N^{-\sigma}) = O(N^{-1}) — a full extra factor of N^{-1+\sigma} faster. A truncated sum settles down noticeably quicker near a real zero than it does anywhere else. That asymmetry is the whole engine of the argument.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that \zeta has a zero off the critical line at \sigma=\tfrac12-u (u\ne0), height t. This isn’t an extra assumption smuggled in — it’s forced by two completely standard facts: \zeta(\bar s)=\overline{\zeta(s)} (real coefficients), and \zeta(s)=\chi(s)\zeta(1-s) (the functional equation, with \chi(s) = \pi^{s-1/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)/\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{2}\right), the same factor used throughout this series since Chapter 8). Chain them together and an off-line zero at \tfrac12-u+it drags a mirror zero along with it, at \tfrac12+u+it — same height, reflected across the line. So if an off-line zero exists at all, both \mu(\tfrac12-u+it)=0 and \mu(\tfrac12+u+it)=0 hold simultaneously, and both symmetric points get the enhanced O(N^{-1}) decay above.

Separately — and this is the piece that does the real work — suppose \psi_N obeys an approximate-functional-equation-type relation of its own, mirroring \chi:

\psi_N(1-s) \;=\; \frac{N^{1-2s}}{\chi(s)}\,\psi_N(s).

At the enhanced-decay case, both sides are O(N^{-1}) by the paragraph above — plug that order directly into the relation and the N^{1-2s} factor has nowhere to go except to force 1-2\sigma=0. \sigma=\tfrac12. No off-line zero can produce a consistent order on both sides of its own approximate functional equation unless it sits exactly on the line.

That’s Take Four, in full. It’s the cleanest version of the six-times-recurring argument this project has produced — and it very nearly has no moving parts left to hide a gap in.

Try it

Pick a height. Every point plotted is a real value of k(t) from Chapter 5, computed at the exact midpoint between two consecutive genuine zeros of \zeta (not illustrative — from mpmath.zetazero) — the same quantity that resurfaces below as the actual reason this argument’s last step is hard.

Where the bug actually was

I did not take the “\psi_N obeys its own approximate functional equation” step on faith. Checked directly, numerically, against the actual object as written above (\mu_N, plain rectangle-truncation, no correction term): its functional-equation defect is 500–30,000× larger than it should be for the argument to close, and — worse — the defect doesn’t shrink to zero as N\to\infty at fixed t, which is exactly the regime this argument needs it in. As literally written, the premise never actually holds, at any scale.

The fix turned out to be small and completely standard: every other object in this project’s toolkit — f(s)=\sum_{n=1}^M n^{-s}+\tfrac12(M+1)^{-s} from Chapter 4 onward — carries a half-weighted trapezoidal correction at the truncation point that \mu_N(s) above simply omits. Swapping it in, f_N(s) := \mu_N(s) + \tfrac12 N^{-s}, and rechecking: the ratio of the functional-equation defect to the enhanced-decay signal it needs to stay smaller than shrinks to zero cleanly and monotonically as N\to\infty, exactly as required. The mechanism is nothing exotic — the trapezoidal rule is one order more accurate than the plain rectangle rule, full stop — but it’s the difference between a premise that’s actually true and one that only looked true from a distance. With f_N in place of \mu_N, Take Four’s functional-equation step is on solid ground.

The step that’s still an assumption, precisely

Fixing the bug above doesn’t fix the argument’s real gap, and I want to be exactly as specific about it as the site’s roadmap promised. Look again at the order-matching step: it concludes \sigma=\tfrac12 by assuming the leading coefficients on both sides of the rescaled functional equation are genuinely nonzero — i.e. that the O(N^{-1}) terms don’t hide an accidental extra cancellation that would let the equation balance at some \sigma\ne\tfrac12 too, at a lower order still. Nothing in the derivation above rules that out. It’s asserted by omission, not derived — and that is precisely the same move every other version of this argument has made, going back to 2023’s Jensen-convexity pairing, dressed differently each time. The site’s own outline calls it “the same core monotonicity/remainder-bound gap,” and Take Four doesn’t close it — it just states it in its sharpest form yet: why can’t a symmetric pair of near-zero values conspire to cancel at extra order, at some \sigma\ne\tfrac12?

That question has another name, already sitting in this project’s own notes.

Where Chapter 5 finishes the job

Chapter 5’s transverse curvature k(t) is exactly a ban on that kind of conspiracy — a lower bound on how sharply |\zeta(\sigma+it)|^2 has to curve upward as \sigma moves off \tfrac12, ruling out precisely the extra-order cancellation Take Four needs excluded. Write L(s) := \zeta'(s)/\zeta(s), the logarithmic derivative — completely standard, and, remarkably, absent by name from three years of these notes despite the project attacking \zeta from every other direction. A short calculus exercise, checked numerically against \zeta directly to 11 decimal places, gives k(t) an exact closed form:

k(t) \;=\; 2\,\mathrm{Re}\big[L'(\tfrac12+it)\big] \;+\; 4\,\big[\mathrm{Re}\,L(\tfrac12+it)\big]^2.

L(s) has its own classical expansion — the Hadamard product for \xi(s)=\tfrac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)\zeta(s) gives L(s) as a smooth, explicit O(\log t) term plus a sum of 1/(s-\rho) over every nontrivial zero \rho=\tfrac12+i\gamma (writing \gamma for a zero’s height, the way t is this chapter’s own height variable). Plugged into the boxed formula, the nearest zeros on the line dominate: each contributes 2/(t-\gamma)^2 to k(t), so k(t) is, to good approximation, the reciprocal-squared distance to the nearest zero. Checked directly against real data (mpmath.zetazero, midpoints between consecutive zeros):

n t_{\text{mid}} local gap mean-gap prediction k(t) k(t)/\log^2 t
1 17.6 6.89 6.11 1.51 0.18
20 78.2 2.19 2.49 10.40 0.55
100 237.1 1.25 1.73 24.67 0.83
500 812.0 1.59 1.29 32.31 0.72
2000 2515.9 1.28 1.05 49.56 0.81

k(t)/\log^2t settles into a stable band rather than drifting toward 0, consistent with the hypothesis — and, tellingly, the widest local gap in the table (n=500) doesn’t produce an unusually high ratio, exactly as expected if k(t) tracks the nearest zero’s true distance rather than the average spacing. A wider stress test against the first two million known zeros, across nine orders of magnitude in height, shows the same picture: no case found where k(t)/\log^2t trends toward zero, even at the widest gaps on record.

So Take Four’s missing step and Chapter 5’s open hypothesis are the same statement: no two zeros on the line are ever allowed to sit closer together than the typical spacing suggests, at any height, ever. That is not a warm-up lemma. It is a uniform, for-every-t statement about how zeros of \zeta can cluster — and statements of that shape have a name in the literature.

The name

Goldston, Gonek and Montgomery — Mean values of the logarithmic derivative of the Riemann zeta-function with applications to primes in short intervals, J. Reine Angew. Math. 537 (2001) — proved that the sharp mean-square asymptotic of L(s) shifted just off the critical line (the exact limit k(t) reduces to) is one of three canonical integrals whose sharp asymptotic is equivalent to Montgomery’s Pair Correlation Conjecture. (Chandee’s survey “A tale of three integrals,” arXiv:2108.09258, states all three side by side; Fan Ge’s 2023 follow-up, arXiv:2211.08571, sharpens the picture further but still needs RH plus an explicit small-gaps hypothesis.)

So: an unconditional, for-every-t proof of k(t)\ge c_0\log^2t wouldn’t just be hard. It would resolve a named, fifty-year-old open conjecture about how zeros of \zeta correlate with each other — squarely in Montgomery–Odlyzko/GUE-statistics territory, not “elementary argument waiting to be found” territory. That’s the honest answer to a question this project has asked six times in six vocabularies since 2023: why does the same wall keep reappearing? Because it isn’t a wall specific to any one argument. It’s one wall, with a name, and every reformulation in this project — the Moon’s Jensen pairing, the stationary-phase endpoint argument, Take Four’s order-matching, Chapter 5’s curvature — was climbing the same side of it.

What this does and doesn’t do

This does not prove the Riemann Hypothesis, and it does not prove k(t)\ge c_0\log^2t either — if anything, it explains precisely why neither this project nor anyone else has. What it does do: convert three years of “I keep hitting the same wall, worded differently each time” into a single, precise, citable statement of which open problem that wall actually is. That’s a real answer, even though it isn’t the answer I set out looking for in 2020.

It also leaves a genuinely open door, and I want to name it rather than let the chapter end on pure closure. The unconditional-for-every-t version of k(t)\ge c_0\log^2t is PCC-equivalent — off the table with current technology. A weaker, almost-all-t statistical version — true for a density-one set of heights in [T,2T], not every single one — is a different kind of question, and classical second- and fourth-moment theorems for \zeta and its derivatives (Ingham’s theorem among them) are close in shape to what such a proof would need. I haven’t built it. But for the first time in three years, I know exactly which hundred-year-old toolbox to open first.
