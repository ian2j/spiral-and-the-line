Chapter 4: Pre-tail and Tail

By the fall of 2025, two pieces from earlier chapters were sitting next to each other without quite touching: the spiral picture from Chapter 2, where partial sums of \zeta(s) wind toward a fixed center, and the endpoint-vs-saddle deformation trick from Chapter 3, which manufactures a genuine stationary point out of a boundary-only integral by adding a quadratic term to the phase and analytically continuing it away again. This chapter is where those two pieces actually fused — and where M = \lfloor t/\pi \rfloor, the truncation length used everywhere from here on, stopped being a number I’d noticed worked well and became a number I could explain.

Why \lfloor t/\pi \rfloor, precisely

Look at a single term of the alternating series at height t: (-1)^{n+1} n^{-\sigma-it}. Its phase, as a function of n, is \phi(n) = t\log n - \pi n (the \pi n comes from folding the alternating sign into the exponential, the same move Chapter 1 made with cosines and sines). This phase has a genuine stationary point — no deformation required this time — exactly where \phi'(n) = t/n - \pi = 0, which is n = t/\pi. There’s no trick here, no \alpha \to 0 limit: this stationary point is really there, sitting inside the sum, for every height t. If you’re going to split a partial sum into “the part before something interesting happens” and “the part after,” this is the honest place to make the cut.

The split

Define the finite pre-tail — a partial sum of \zeta, run out just past that stationary point, with a half-weighted final term for good measure:

P(s) = \zeta_M(s) + \tfrac12 (M+1)^{-s}, \qquad M = \lfloor t/\pi \rfloor.

(This is the same object that reappears, unchanged, as f(s) in Chapter 8 — I hadn’t settled on final notation yet in 2025, but the definition never moved again after this.) Partial sums of \zeta spiral toward a fixed center as more terms are added, and P(s) turns out to be a genuinely good approximation to that center — because n = t/\pi is close to where consecutive terms’ magnitudes are comparable and their phases point in nearly opposite directions, which is exactly the point where a spiral’s trajectory is closest to having already settled.

The tail is everything after M: T(s) = \eta_N(s) - \eta_M(s), for the alternating series this time, and it’s where Chapter 3’s machinery earns its keep. Treated as a Fresnel-type integral around that same stationary point, the tail collapses to a closed form:

T(s) \;\approx\; 2^{-1/2}\left(\frac{t}{\pi}\right)^{\frac12 - \sigma} \exp\!\left(i\left[\frac{5\pi}{4} - t\log\frac{t}{\pi} + t\right]\right).

Notice what happens to the magnitude at \sigma = \tfrac12 specifically: the exponent \tfrac12 - \sigma vanishes, and \left(t/\pi\right)^0 = 1 for every height t. The tail’s magnitude collapses to exactly 2^{-1/2} — a single universal constant, independent of where on the critical line you are. Off the critical line, that magnitude drifts with t; on it, it doesn’t.

What this predicts at a zero

The eta and zeta functions are related by \eta(s) = (1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s), and the same relation holds, approximately, between their pre-tail approximations. Combine that with the split above:

\eta_M(s) = \eta_N(s) - T(s) = (1-2^{1-s})P(s) - T(s).

If s is a genuine zero — so that both the true spiral center and its finite approximation P(s) vanish — this collapses to a clean prediction:

\eta_M(s) \;\approx\; -T(s).

The pre-tail sum, at a real zero, should point in almost exactly the opposite direction from the tail’s closed form, with almost exactly the same magnitude, 2^{-1/2}.

Try it

Pick a known zero and watch the two vectors, drawn tip to tail. Nudge \sigma off the critical line and watch the cancellation break.

Checking it against 100,000 real zeros

This is a concrete, falsifiable claim about actual known numbers, so I checked it against actual known numbers: the first 100,000 nontrivial zeros of \zeta, all of them verified to sit on the critical line. In every case, the pre-tail sum \eta_M(s) and the tail estimate T(s) come out pointing in nearly opposite directions, with modulus close to 1/\sqrt2, exactly as predicted.

I want to be as precise about this as I was at the time: “I have no way of currently proving rigorously that this is always the case for all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function.” This is a strong, clean, checkable numerical regularity — a hundred thousand data points is not nothing — but it is numerics, not a proof, and I want to keep those two things visibly separate rather than let the size of the check do the work a derivation should.

How fast does the match improve with height, and why: this went unquantified for a long time too — “checked, all consistent” doesn’t say how consistent, or whether it’s getting better. It is: |\eta_M(s)| - 2^{-1/2}, measured directly against the same 100,000 zeros, shrinks like t^{-1/2} — confirmed by tracking \lvert\,|\eta_M(s)|-2^{-1/2}\,\rvert\cdot\sqrt t across the full height range (t=21 to t\approx75{,}000), which stays in a bounded band rather than drifting. The reason traces back to the pre-tail construction itself: P(s) doesn’t exactly vanish at a zero the way the derivation above assumes — a genuine spiral-geometry term (N^{1-s}/(1-s), Chapter 2’s discovery) survives even though \zeta(s)=0, and it’s this order-t^{-1/2} term, not numerical noise, that sets the pace. Tried folding that term back into the prediction directly to sharpen it further; it didn’t help — the residual error didn’t shrink, meaning T(s)’s own approximation carries a comparable, uncorrected error of its own. So: the rate is now derived, not just observed, but tightening the match further needs a second correction (to T(s), not P(s)) that hasn’t been worked out yet.

Whatever happens to the rest of this project, I think this result stands on its own: a closed-form prediction about the geometry of truncated zeta and eta sums near real zeros, derived from first principles, checked at scale, and still — as far as I know — unexplained by anything deeper than “the numbers do this.”
