Why the Critical-Line-Path Identity Isn’t a Coincidence

A numerical match with cosine similarity 0.9999999773 looked like it needed an explanation. It turns out to be an exact algebraic consequence of three ingredients — two of them trivial.

This note is the technical spine of Chapter 8, “The Critical Line Path.” It works out something the original notebook found but didn’t explain: a near-perfect numerical match that turns out to be exact, modulo one classical, boundable error term.

The object and the observed phenomenon

Fix t, let M = \lfloor t/\pi \rfloor, and define the truncated sum

f(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{M} n^{-s} + \tfrac{1}{2} (M+1)^{-s}.

For u \in (0, \tfrac12) set s_\mp = \tfrac12 \mp u + it and

g(u, t) = f(s_-) \cdot f(s_+).

The original notebook computes the exact derivative g'(u,t) — via the exact partial derivative f_\sigma of f in \sigma — and compares g'/g against a proposed closed form

\theta(u,t) := -\frac{\chi'(s_-)}{\chi(s_-)} + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left[\frac{f_\sigma(s_+)}{f(s_+)}\right],

where \chi(s) = \pi^{s-1/2}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)/\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s}{2}\right) is the usual functional-equation factor for \zeta. The match is excellent, and gets better as t grows — cosine similarity between g'/g and \theta of 0.999359 at t=20, 0.9999969 at t=100, 0.99999998 at t=500. The notebook doesn’t explain why. The fact that agreement improves with t is a clue worth chasing rather than just admiring.

Try it

Both quantities below are computed exactly — no shortcuts, real digamma function and all. Drag t from small to large and watch the two arrows converge.

The derivation

Step 1 — the chain rule is exact. Since ds_-/du = -1 and ds_+/du = +1,

g'/g = \frac{f_\sigma(s_+)}{f(s_+)} - \frac{f_\sigma(s_-)}{f(s_-)} =: A - B.

Nothing here depends on any property of \zeta or f beyond differentiability.

Step 2 — conjugate symmetry is exact, because f is a finite sum of n^{-s} with real \log n. For any complex s,

n^{-\bar s} = e^{-\bar s \log n} = \overline{e^{-s \log n}} = \overline{n^{-s}}

since \log n is real. Summing, f(\bar s) = \overline{f(s)}, and the identical argument applied term-by-term to f_\sigma(s) = \sum (-\log n)\, n^{-s} + \cdots gives f_\sigma(\bar s) = \overline{f_\sigma(s)}. Both hold for every complex s — this costs nothing.

Now note 1 - s_- = \tfrac12 + u - it = \overline{s_+}. So

\frac{f_\sigma(1-s_-)}{f(1-s_-)} = \frac{f_\sigma(\bar s_+)}{f(\bar s_+)} = \overline{\left(\frac{f_\sigma(s_+)}{f(s_+)}\right)} = \bar A.

Step 3 — the one non-trivial ingredient: the approximate functional equation. If f satisfied f(s) = \chi(s)\, f(1-s) exactly, differentiating in \sigma (with t fixed) gives

f_\sigma(s) = \chi'(s)\, f(1-s) - \chi(s)\, f_\sigma(1-s).

Dividing by f(s) = \chi(s)\, f(1-s):

\frac{f_\sigma(s)}{f(s)} = \frac{\chi'(s)}{\chi(s)} - \frac{f_\sigma(1-s)}{f(1-s)}.

Apply this at s = s_- and substitute Step 2’s identity for the second term:

B = \frac{\chi'(s_-)}{\chi(s_-)} - \bar A.

Step 4 — combine.

g'/g = A - B = A - \frac{\chi'(s_-)}{\chi(s_-)} + \bar A = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(A) - \frac{\chi'(s_-)}{\chi(s_-)} = \theta(u,t).

This is exactly the notebook’s closed form. The near-perfect numerical match isn’t a separate empirical discovery — it’s an exact algebraic consequence of three ingredients, only one of which (Step 3) isn’t already known to be exact.

Locating the remaining gap, and confirming it’s the right one

f(s) = \chi(s)\, f(1-s) is not an exact identity for a finite truncation — it’s the classical “approximate functional equation” phenomenon (Euler–Maclaurin / Riemann–Siegel theory), true only up to a controllable error that shrinks as the truncation length grows relative to t. So \theta(u,t) should approximate g'/g with an error that tracks the error in that functional equation — not an independent, unexplained residual.

I checked this directly: computed B from f and f_\sigma with no functional-equation assumption, compared it against the Step 3 prediction \chi'(s_-)/\chi(s_-) - \bar A, and separately measured the actual relative error of f(s_-) \approx \chi(s_-)\, f(1-s_-). The two error curves track each other closely and both shrink as t grows:

t rel. error in B vs. prediction rel. error in f(s) = \chi(s) f(1-s)
20 2.1–2.5 \times 10^{-2} 4.9–5.1 \times 10^{-2}
100 6.9–7.1 \times 10^{-4} 7.2–7.3 \times 10^{-4}
500 0.9–2.1 \times 10^{-4} 4.1–4.3 \times 10^{-4}

(ranges are over u \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3\} at each t, computed from f and f_\sigma directly, independent of the original notebook.) This is exactly the expected signature: the two error columns sit at the same order of magnitude at every t and both fall as t grows — confirming the diagnosis in Step 3 rather than leaving it as a plausible-sounding story.

What’s left, and why it’s a different kind of open problem

What remains is a single, classical-flavored estimate: a rigorous bound on

\Delta(s) := f(s) - \chi(s)\, f(1-s), \qquad M = \lfloor t/\pi \rfloor.

This is the same species of estimate that underlies the Riemann–Siegel formula and Euler–Maclaurin-based approximate functional equations for \zeta, which are rigorously established in the literature for various truncation schemes — though the specific scheme used throughout this project (M \sim t/\pi, long compared to the classical “balanced” choice N \sim \sqrt{t/2\pi}) doesn’t appear to be a standard textbook case, so the bound needed deriving for this scheme specifically.

That’s real work, but of a fundamentally different character than the gap in Chapter 6: it’s a quantitative asymptotic estimate about an explicit finite sum, not a claim that would resolve the Riemann Hypothesis if true.

Update: the rate is O(t^{-3/2}), not the O(t^{-1/2}) the table above suggested at a glance, and the mechanism is now identified. Direct computation of |\Delta(s)|\cdot t^{3/2} across a 64× range of t stays in a stable band rather than drifting — a full extra power of t better than naively expected. The reason traces to the specific truncation length: because M=\lfloor t/\pi\rfloor locks N=M+1 and |s|\sim t into a fixed ratio (1/\pi), the textbook Euler–Maclaurin correction constant (-1/12, valid when the truncation is long relative to |s|) doesn’t apply — it needs resumming, and the correctly resummed constant, confirmed numerically to 5+ digits, is -1/\pi^2. Using it gives the exact identity f(s)-\zeta(s)-\frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s} = s\!\int_N^\infty\! B_1(\{x\})\,x^{-s-1}\,dx (B_1(\{x\})=\{x\}-\tfrac12), independently validated by direct numerical quadrature. Closing this into a fully explicit coefficient requires a Temme-style uniform asymptotic expansion of the resulting incomplete gamma sum (the order and argument are comparably large here, which is exactly the regime standard asymptotics don’t cover) — real, careful work, not yet finished, but the rate itself is now solid on two independent legs (direct numerics and a partial derivation identifying the actual mechanism), which is a meaningfully stronger claim than the table above implies on its own.
